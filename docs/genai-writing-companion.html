Use AI to improve your writing, not just create more of it, with a four-stage framework from blank page to finished piece.

The big idea: a chatbot is a fast, enthusiastic intern. Use it to generate quantity at each stage of writing, then use your own judgment to choose, trim and edit. The real problem in writing is getting someone to read your words, not getting words onto a page.

This page accompanies a live three-hour course on using generative AI chatbots to help with writing. It summarises what the instructor taught. It does not replace the course slides.

Principles

  1. Chatbots can make mistakes. The instructor suggests treating one like a well-informed stranger you meet in the street: probably useful, but double-check anything you rely on.
  2. Generative AI is an intern, not a deity. It is enthusiastic and very productive, but the average quality of its work is a little below that of an equally well-informed human.
  3. No response, and so no prompt, is perfect. However much detail you cram into a prompt, expect a few mistakes.
  4. Ask for options. Chatbots are good at quantity, humans are good at quality. Combining the two makes you more productive and your work better.
  5. Conversations are more powerful than prompts. Like an intern, a chatbot can be steered back on course as it goes wrong, which is harder with one huge set of instructions.
  6. You are the author. The chatbot is a writing assistant. Whatever you keep from it, you chose and you are responsible for.
  7. Solve the right problem. Writing is not about getting words down. It is about getting somebody else to read them, so use AI to make writing better, not just bigger.

The main frameworks

Seven parts of a prompt

Role, task, format, tone, audience, scope and things to include. A role asks the chatbot to talk in a certain way; it does not make it an expert. Most prompts use only one to three parts. Giving examples is sometimes called few-shot prompting.

Repeat and refine

Ask for ten options, pick the promising one, add scope or feedback, and ask again. It is a broad, shallow search that you steer. It works because responses vary from run to run.

The four writing stages

Think of building a body: ideas are the skeleton, a motivated outline brings it to life, trimming reduces the fat, and the write-up puts skin on the bones. The instructor says this is one way to write, so use what helps.

Head, heart, behaviour ideas

Aristotle's three modes of persuasion (logos, pathos, ethos) as sources of ideas: things you can know, things you can feel, things you can do. Ask a chatbot for ten of each, including ethical ones.

Motivated outline

A bullet outline in which each major section solves a problem for the reader. A problem helps the content get past the brain's filter. The OODA sequence (observe, orient, decide, act) can break a problem down.

The 10-minute rule

A rule of thumb, not a law: attention lasts about ten minutes, roughly four to five pages of text. Split longer sections, each with its own problem.

Tentpole sentences

For a document, write your key thoughts in order as bullets under an outline point. The chatbot expands each into a paragraph. You control the shape and then edit the text.

Box-outs and basic global English

Box-outs split a long text into small panels that skimmers can scan. Basic global English is plainer language that is easier for international readers and often improves the writing.

Worked examples from the session

The outputs shown live in the course were illustrations of the techniques, not verified facts. Do not reuse them as data.

Marathon plan and disaster recovery plan

A prompt using all seven parts, then a simpler one asking a personal trainer persona for a weekly marathon schedule in an enthusiastic tone. Both showed better results with more detail, but still not perfect ones.

Names and documentary ideas

Repeat and refine was used to find a YouTube channel name and to brainstorm a trivial Netflix documentary. Asking for ten, then ten more, then narrowing with scope moved towards ideas such as a series about airport carpets.

Head, heart and behaviour ideas, then a mind map

Factual, emotional and ethical ideas about a disaster recovery plan were requested, and a React talk was planned on a hand-drawn mind map with three branches. Most of that talk came from one highlighted area of the map.

Competitive analysis with a deep research mode

A research-style chatbot rated consultancies on vision and ability to execute. One source looked like an SEO listicle when checked against the CRAAP criteria (currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, purpose).

Dictation to outline to motivated outline

A transcription of the instructor talking through four levels of React state became an outline, then each section was given a problem and a four-step arc.

Slides, documents and styles

An outline became a PowerPoint file with three-word bullets. A company report became box-outs. A short announcement was rewritten in the styles of a Peckham-flavoured Pride and Prejudice and of a compliment-giving comedian.

Interactive graphics

A spreadsheet of pastimes by US state became a clickable web map built by a chatbot, then one with county detail, a dark mode and drawing tools. The suggestion is to try something like this for presentations.

Cautions

  • Privacy. The instructor warned that chat services may use your conversations for training. Check the settings and your organisation's rules before pasting anything private.
  • Bias and sources. A chatbot answering from its core learning knows things without knowing why. Ask for references to push it to search, and be aware that a chatbot may be built with a point of view. Check sources, for example with the CRAAP criteria.
  • Expanded text can be wrong. When a chatbot expands a bullet, it may use its core memory, which can misremember. Edit and check facts.
  • Too much motivation. A long, elaborate scenario can become more interesting than the topic, so readers miss what they should learn.
  • Pictures. AI-generated illustrations are increasingly detectable by viewers and, according to the instructor, no longer add value as they once did.
  • Detecting AI text. The instructor's view is that detectors are unreliable and are being outpaced by AI-generated text. He argues that signatures would be more trustworthy than watermarks. This is his opinion, not a settled result.
  • Things said informally. Dates, laws, product features, research references and attributions mentioned in passing may be misremembered or out of date. Verify before repeating them.

Glossary

Prompt and response
A prompt is anything you type into a chatbot. Anything you get back is a response.
Few-shot prompting
Giving the chatbot a number of examples of what you mean instead of an abstract rule.
Repeat and refine
Asking for options, filtering them yourself and asking again with more guidance.
Core learning
What a chatbot picked up in training, as opposed to what it looks up when searching.
Deep research mode
A mode where a chatbot searches many sources and writes a report with references.
CRAAP criteria
Currency, relevance, authority, accuracy and purpose: five checks for judging a source.
Mind map
A diagram with a topic in the centre and ideas on branches, used here to focus and integrate ideas.
Transcription
A text version of speech, used to turn your talking into material a chatbot can outline.
Motivated outline
A bullet outline whose major sections each solve a problem for the reader.
Arc
One problem being solved, roughly one section of the writing.
OODA
Observe, orient, decide, act: four steps used to describe a problem being solved.
Globus pallidus
A part of the brain the instructor described as controlling access to working memory, used as a picture of why dull material feels boring.
10-minute rule
A rule of thumb that a section should take no more than about ten minutes or four to five pages.
Demoted material
Content moved out of the main piece into an appendix or another document.
Tentpole sentences
Your key thoughts in order, which hold up the shape of the text a chatbot expands.
Basic global English
A simplified form of English with a smaller vocabulary and simpler grammar.
Box-outs
Small separate panels that run alongside the main text so that readers can skim.
Marp
A text format for slides that can be converted into a PowerPoint file.
Stylometry
Statistical analysis of writing style, used by some AI-text detectors.
Watermark and signature
A watermark marks content in a way that can be removed. A signature is a checkable code tied to unchanged content.
