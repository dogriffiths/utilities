Research.

How to get better answers from chatbots, and how to check the answers you get.

The big idea: treat a chatbot as a well-read research intern. Sharpen your question, check its plan, verify its sources, and make the final judgment yourself.

This page accompanies a live one-hour course on using generative AI chatbots to research a topic. It summarises what the instructor taught. It does not replace the course slides, which hold the full prompts.

Principles

  1. A chatbot is an intern, not a deity. It is not all-knowing. In research or thinking mode it is an intern with a plan: more structure, but the same basic intern.
  2. You are the researcher. The chatbot is your assistant in the library stacks. It fetches and summarises, but the research is still yours, so do not lift its report and send it out under your name.
  3. Prefer sources you can check. A chatbot answering from its training cannot say why it believes something. One connected to search can show where information came from.
  4. Sharpen the question before you run it. Vague queries, gaps and hidden assumptions all lower the quality of the result.
  5. Check the plan, then the sources, then the logic. Review what the chatbot intends to do before it does it, and read the finished report in depth.
  6. Look for agreement across attempts. Run a prompt more than once and on more than one chatbot. When different tools reach similar conclusions you can have a little more confidence.
  7. Credibility matters more than detection. For AI-generated content, the instructor would spend more time verifying a source than trying to prove that something was written by AI.

The main frameworks

How research mode works

Understand the question, plan, gather (often in repeated loops), synthesise by plugging gaps and resolving contradictions, then draft and verify. Many chatbots now switch this mode on automatically when a question looks like it needs more work.

PICO

A format from medical research: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. Ask the chatbot to question you, then rewrite your query in this shape. It works for non-medical questions too.

Check the plan

Before a deep research run, ask the chatbot to explain its plan. Look especially at which sources it will use, and tighten the query, for example by excluding social media and vendors’ own sites.

CRAAP criteria

Five tests for a source: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy and Purpose. Purpose is where the instructor says the real issues often show, such as affiliate income or a vendor’s own marketing.

Analysis of Competing Hypotheses

For “why” questions. Generate several explanations, then weigh each against the evidence and rank them. Useful when you do not yet have a theory.

Key Assumptions Check

For when you already have a theory. List the assumptions it rests on, test each one, and find the weakest link. It can also correct a mistaken premise, such as a wrong date.

Indicators and signposts

Stress-test a plan or forecast by asking what observable signs would show it is going wrong.

Detecting AI-generated content

Watermarks and metadata are easy to strip from images. Text detectors using stylometry give probabilities and can misjudge careful non-native writers. Geometry checks can expose some images.

Worked examples from the session

The outputs shown live in the course were illustrations of the techniques, not verified facts. Do not reuse them as data.

Core learning versus search

A locally run chatbot answers from what it absorbed in training, which is fast and works offline but can contain significant errors. A question about recent events cannot be answered that way, so the chatbot has to search, which also gives it sources.

HR software query

A bare question about the best HR software produced a generic list. After answering the chatbot’s questions, the query was rewritten in PICO form for small UK businesses, the plan was reviewed, and vendor sites and social media were excluded before the report was run.

Checking a source with CRAAP

One cited source scored well on relevance but, on purpose, appeared to be a comparison site earning affiliate income, with its claims tracing back to the vendor’s own marketing. The instructor also described using the same idea to check the background of an investment-course seller.

Ice cream sales drop

A spreadsheet showed an outlet’s sales falling sharply in late July. Several chatbots proposed similar hypotheses, with the end of a nearby major sporting event ranked as most likely, ahead of weather and reporting changes.

Gold price theory

A key assumptions check on a theory about a gold price rise corrected the date and found that the proposed cause was supported only in part, because other factors were also in play.

Planning 25% more production

An indicators-and-signposts prompt, given the same sales data, flagged that the earlier boost looked event-driven, that the autumn event calendar was thin and that falling temperatures would hurt demand. The chatbots advised caution.

A ballroom photograph

Lines that should be parallel in a rectangular room were extended to see whether they met at a common vanishing point. They did not, which suggested the image was not a true photograph.

Cautions

  • Privacy. The instructor said conversations may be used for training by default and that, even with the training setting off, they are likely still stored. Treat a chatbot like email and do not enter anything you would be unhappy to see published. He is not a security professional; ask someone suitably qualified.
  • Invented references. A lawyer in a New York court case relied on a chatbot-written brief that cited cases and links that did not exist. Click every link.
  • Hypotheses are guesses. A competing-hypotheses run invents explanations. Results vary each time, so repeat and compare across tools.
  • Detectors can mislead. Text detectors are statistical, are fairly easy to fool by asking for simpler language, and can flag formal human writing. Absence of a watermark does not show that something was written by a human.
  • Informal claims. Historical and legal asides (the origin of CRAAP, the use of the CIA primer by other agencies, the date and scope of the EU labelling rule) were told from memory. Verify them before repeating them.
  • Tool availability changes. Menu names and settings shown in the session may have changed since.

Glossary

Prompt and query
Anything you type to a chatbot is a prompt; in research work people tend to call it a query. What comes back is the response.
Core learning
The instructor’s term for what a chatbot retains from its training. It works like a compressed, lossy copy of the internet and can fill gaps by inventing.
Research, deep research or thinking mode
A slower mode in which the chatbot plans, searches repeatedly and synthesises before writing a report.
Gather stage
The part of research mode where the chatbot generates searches and records findings and their sources.
Synthesis
Combining the gathered findings, plugging gaps and resolving contradictions.
PICO
Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome: a medical-research format for framing a question.
CRAAP
Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose: a checklist for judging a source.
Tradecraft Primer
A published CIA document of structured analytical techniques for reasoning about why things happen.
Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)
Comparing several possible explanations against the evidence to rank them.
Key Assumptions Check
Testing each assumption behind an existing theory.
Indicators and signposts
Observable signs that a plan or forecast is going wrong.
Informational heat death
The idea that content generated from chatbot output, fed back into training, gets steadily worse in quality.
Watermark
A visible mark or hidden metadata showing content was generated by AI. Easy to remove from images.
Stylometry
A statistical way of comparing writing style, used by many AI-text detectors.
Vanishing point
The point where parallel lines in a 3D scene appear to meet in a photograph.
Signature
A cryptographic sign-off by a publisher, which puts its credibility behind the content.

Principles

  • The chatbot is an intern, not a deity
  • You are the researcher
  • Prefer answers with sources
  • Repeat and compare across chatbots

Search versus core learning

  • Core learning: fast, offline, can invent
  • Search: current and gives sources
  • Research mode: plan, gather, synthesise, draft and verify

Sharpen the query

  1. Ask the chatbot to question you
  2. Remove vagueness, gaps and assumptions
  3. Rewrite in PICO form
  4. Ask for its plan before running

PICO

  • Population: who or what
  • Intervention: what you are trying
  • Comparison: against what
  • Outcome: what you expect

Check the plan

  • Which sources will it use?
  • Exclude social media or vendor sites if needed
  • Ask for peer-reviewed or respected sources
  • Update the query, then run

Check the report

  • Click every link
  • Read the logic in depth
  • Check each source with CRAAP
  • Treat the report as a theory

CRAAP

  • Currency: up to date?
  • Relevance: right topic?
  • Authority: who is behind it?
  • Accuracy: evidence disclosed?
  • Purpose: how do they make money?

Which technique?

  • No theory yet: Analysis of Competing Hypotheses
  • Have a theory: Key Assumptions Check
  • Have a plan or forecast: indicators and signposts

Competing hypotheses

  1. Give the event and the data
  2. Ask for three hypotheses
  3. Assess each against the evidence
  4. Rank them
  5. Repeat on other chatbots

Key Assumptions Check

  • Is the event itself true?
  • Is each supporting claim evidenced?
  • Is the cause the sole or main one?
  • Look for corrections to your premise

Spotting AI images

  • Do not rely on watermarks or metadata
  • Extend lines that should be parallel
  • Do they meet at a common vanishing point?
  • Inconsistent points are a warning sign

Spotting AI text

  • Detectors give probabilities only
  • They can flag formal human writing
  • Simplifying the text can fool them
  • Verify the source instead
