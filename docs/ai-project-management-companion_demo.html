The big idea: treat a chatbot as a fast, enthusiastic intern. Let it produce quantity, then use your own judgment to filter, edit and decide.
Principles Frameworks Worked examples Cautions Glossary

This page accompanies a live three-hour course on using generative AI chatbots in project management. It summarises what was taught. It does not replace the course slides, which hold the full prompts.

Principles

  1. Chatbots can make mistakes. Treat one like a well-informed person you meet at the water cooler: probably right, but check any fact before relying on it.
  2. A chatbot is an intern, not a deity. It is well read, confident and very productive, but its work is a little below what an equally informed human produces.
  3. Ask for options. Chatbots are good at quantity; humans are good at spotting quality. Combining them is what the course calls co-intelligence.
  4. Conversations are more powerful than prompts. Steer the chatbot as you read its answers instead of trying to write one perfect prompt.
  5. No response, and so no prompt, is perfect. Expect to edit, especially when a team will share the result.
  6. Use transcriptions. Turn speech (meetings, your own dictation) into text, then let the chatbot work on the text.
  7. Check the arithmetic. Chatbots find turning words into numbers easy, but multiplying numbers harder.

The main frameworks

Seven parts of a prompt

Role, task, format, tone, audience, scope and things to include. A role gives the chatbot a point of view; it does not make it an expert. Most prompts use only one to three parts.

Repeat and refine

Ask for options, filter them with your own judgment, give feedback, and repeat. Like a broad, shallow search through idea space.

Tentpole sentences

You write your key thoughts, in order. The chatbot expands each into a paragraph. You then edit. You control the shape; it supplies the volume.

Box-outs

Summarise a long document as small, separate information panels so readers can jump to the parts they care about.

Head, heart, behaviour ideas

Aristotle's modes of persuasion used as a prompt: facts and logic, emotion, and character or behaviour. Ask for ten of each.

Likelihood times impact

Score each risk on two 1 to 5 scales and multiply for a significance score. Then ask for mitigations that lower likelihood and mitigations that lower impact.

Worked examples from the session

The outputs shown live in the course were illustrations of the technique, not verified facts. Do not reuse them as data.

Payments epic for a flex-time system

The HereScreen flex-time system needed a payments feature. A chatbot suggested scoping questions; pasted answers produced more edge-case questions; the scope document then fed a request for user stories.

Feature files in Gemini Notebooks

Behaviour-driven scenarios say what a system should do. Loading hundreds of them into a notebook lets you ask questions such as what a flex balance is, with answers citing the scenario fragments they came from. Projects in ChatGPT and Claude work similarly.

Meeting transcript and box-outs

A recorded sales-team call was transcribed with Whisper, then outlined and queried for topics, actions and questions. A long court judgment was turned into box-outs and an HTML page using a skill.

Offshore developers presentation

Ten factual, ten emotional and ten behavioural or ethical ideas were requested, for example the fear of losing control of the development process, then captured on a paper mind map.

Hotel and app reviews

Text reviews were converted to 0 to 10 ratings per aspect, then to a net promoter score per aspect. In a spreadsheet the same approach lets you track app speed, functionality and stability over time.

Risk log for a subscription feature

Text likelihood and impact were converted to 1 to 5 scores and multiplied. Subscription churn and low adoption came out on top; mitigations were then requested for likelihood.

Cautions

  • Privacy. Many chatbots may use your conversations for training unless you switch that off, and that the setting differs between services. Treat a chatbot like email: do not put in anything you would be unhappy to see published. Check with your organisation's security people. For secret material, a locally installed chatbot (for example Ollama) does not send data over the network.
  • Tool availability changes. Statements about which apps, devices or plans support features (such as ChatGPT record mode, skills, or a Copilot function in Excel) were true at the time of the session and may have changed.
  • AI-text detectors. Rewriting in basic global English sometimes defeats statistical detectors, and so detectors should not be trusted to the exclusion of your own judgment.
  • Historical and technical asides (such as the origin of risk as a concept, or how document search works inside Gemini Notebooks) were told informally. Verify before repeating them.

Glossary

Epic
A large piece of work that takes the whole team months, such as building an aircraft hangar.
Scope creep
When you and the product owner come to hold different views of what is included in a piece of work.
User story
A smaller piece of work, derived from the scope, that is valuable in its own right.
Acceptance criteria
Statements that, once all true, mean a story is complete.
INVEST
Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Short, Testable: six checks for a good story.
Behaviour-driven development (BDD)
Writing scenarios in Given, When, Then form describing how a perfect system behaves, then building to satisfy them.
Feature file
A file containing many BDD scenarios; can be run as automated tests.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: a chatbot looks up relevant fragments from a stored document collection before answering. Projects and notebooks use it.
Skill
A packaged set of instructions, and sometimes code, that makes a chatbot repeat a process the same way each time.
Co-intelligence
A human plus a chatbot producing better work than either alone.
Tentpole sentences
Your key thoughts in order, used to shape text a chatbot expands.
Box-outs
Small separate information panels used to break up a complex story.
Transcription
A text version of spoken audio. Tools mentioned include phone recorder apps, Live Transcribe, Whisper and Otter.ai.
Basic global English
A simplified form of English with a smaller vocabulary and fewer tenses.
Mermaid
A text format for diagrams that can be rendered into a chart.
Retrospective
A team session that turns feedback into actions to improve how the project runs.
Affinity groups
Groups of similar feedback items, for example by the aspect of the project they refer to.
Net promoter score
On a 0 to 10 scale, 9 to 10 are promoters, 7 to 8 are passives and 0 to 6 are detractors. The score combines these into a single figure from minus 100 to 100.
Risk score
Likelihood multiplied by impact. With a probability and a money impact it equals expected loss.
Mitigation
An action that reduces a risk's likelihood or its impact.

Prompt parts

  • Role (a point of view)
  • Task
  • Format
  • Tone
  • Audience
  • Scope (rules, limits)
  • Things to include

Repeat and refine

  1. Ask for ten options
  2. Filter with your judgment
  3. Give feedback (for example, include a pun)
  4. Ask for ten more, or ten variants of a favourite

Principles

  • Ask for options
  • Humans judge quality, chatbots supply quantity
  • Conversations beat prompts
  • No response is perfect
  • Use transcriptions

Epic to story

  1. Chatbot suggests questions
  2. You interview; write scope
  3. Chatbot suggests stories
  4. Team chooses the real stories
  5. Check with INVEST
  6. Add acceptance criteria

INVEST

  • Independent (an aim)
  • Negotiable
  • Valuable
  • Estimable
  • Short
  • Testable

Meetings

  • Get a transcript (phone app, recording plus Whisper, or a meeting service)
  • Ask for an outline first, to force a read
  • Then topics, actions and questions

Email and body text

  • Write tentpole sentences
  • Ask for paragraphs in the tone you want
  • Edit heavily
  • Write intros and summaries yourself

Presentation pipeline

  1. Head, heart, behaviour ideas
  2. Paper mind map to find your point
  3. Dictate; chatbot outlines
  4. Edit the outline
  5. Three-word bullets
  6. Export slides; edit

Reports

  • Same ideas and outline steps
  • Expand tentpoles to text
  • Try basic global English for clarity
  • Diagrams: try a Mermaid chart if images come out wrong

Feedback

  • Ask for retrospective formats
  • Group items by aspect
  • Choose one group
  • Ask for five actions per problem

Ratings and NPS

  • Text reviews to 0 to 10 per aspect
  • 9 to 10 promoter
  • 7 to 8 passive
  • 0 to 6 detractor
  • Track in a spreadsheet over time

Risks

  • Score likelihood 1 to 5
  • Score impact 1 to 5
  • Multiply, and check the sums
  • Mitigate likelihood and impact separately
