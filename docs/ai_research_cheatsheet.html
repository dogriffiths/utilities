AI-Assisted Research

Cheat sheet from David Griffiths' "AI-Assisted Research" course

Core Principles

  • You are the researcher — AI is the research intern.
  • AI is great at quantity; humans must supply the quality.
  • Always check references — clicking the link is not optional.
  • Check the logic, not just the facts.
  • Run the same query through multiple chatbots to catch bias.
  • Clarify vagueness, gaps, and hidden assumptions before you search.
  • Judge sources with CRAAP: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
  • Stay skeptical, but stay open-minded.

You Are The Researcher

AI is your intern

  • Productive but not precise: AI generates a huge amount fast; quality is still the human's job.
  • Lossy compression: a chatbot is a compressed version of its training data — like a compressed image, details blur.
  • It doesn't know why it knows: ask for a source and it can't explain — training data carries no citations.
  • Consistency over correctness: chatbots fight to stay consistent with an earlier answer, even a wrong one.
"You are the researcher — AI is the research intern."

Three Levels of AI Research

From instant recall to multi-minute investigation

  1. Core learning: instant answers from training data alone — no internet, no sources.
  2. AI search: the question is rewritten as a web search, then summarized with clickable references.
  3. Research / thinking mode: a slower multi-step process that can take two to three minutes.

AI Search

Who, What, When, and Where

  • Ideal use case: who/what/when/where questions with a single verifiable answer.
  • References are the point: a good AI-search answer comes with links you can actually click.
  • Under the hood: chatbots often quietly route queries through a search engine before summarizing.
  • Still fallible: the summary can misread or misquote the source page.

CIA Structured Analytical Techniques

Why and How

  • Origin: developed after intelligence failures, documented in the CIA's public "Tradecraft Primer."
  • Analysis of Competing Hypotheses: list several explanations, then rank them against the evidence instead of anchoring on the first idea.
  • Counters bias: structured comparison stops implicit assumptions from steering the conclusion.
  • Cross-check chatbots: run the same question through several — each may fixate on a different hypothesis.
  • Best for: "why" and "how" questions, not simple lookups.

Research Mode's Six Stages

What happens inside a "deep research" run

  1. Understand: interprets your prompt (now called a "query") and may ask clarifying questions.
  2. Plan: decides what sources and approach to use.
  3. Gather: runs cascading searches, logging findings and contradictions in a grid.
  4. Synthesize: resolves gaps and conflicting evidence across sources.
  5. Draft & verify: writes a response and runs its own sanity checks.
  6. Deliver: returns a report — still an intern's draft, not PhD-level analysis.

Sharpen Your Query: PICO

Borrowed from medical research to kill vagueness

  • Population: who or what exactly are you studying?
  • Intervention: what's the thing being tried or evaluated?
  • Comparison: what are you measuring it against?
  • Outcome: what specific result actually matters?
  • Ask the bot first: have it list your query's vagueness, gaps, and hidden assumptions before rewriting it in PICO form.

Plan Stage: Name Your Sources

Don't let the model choose blindly

  • Check the plan: ask the chatbot to explain its research plan before it runs.
  • Exclude self-interested sources: for "best product" queries, rule out vendor sites and social media.
  • Why it matters: a "best HR software" search built only from vendor pages will mostly say "we are the best."
  • Update and re-confirm: revise the plan, then explicitly approve it before the research runs.

Verify Everything: Four Checks

The report is a draft for you, not a final deliverable

  • Sources exist: click every link — invented or dead URLs are a real risk.
  • Content matches: confirm the page actually says what the chatbot claims.
  • Logic holds up: check that conclusions actually follow from the evidence.
  • Sources are credible: existing isn't enough — assess reliability too.
Use only what survives your own check.

The CRAAP Test

Five questions for judging any source

  • Currency: is the information current and actively maintained?
  • Relevance: does it actually relate to what you're researching?
  • Authority: who wrote it — a credible voice, or a commercial lead-generator?
  • Accuracy: is there real methodology behind the claims, or just assertions?
  • Purpose: why does this source exist — to inform, or to sell?

The Hallucinated Case Law Warning

Why unchecked citations are dangerous

  • Real incident: a New York lawyer used a chatbot to write a legal brief citing case law.
  • The catch: the URLs to those cases didn't exist — the chatbot invented them.
  • Why it happened: asked to build an argument, the model needed "case law" and generated plausible citations rather than admit it had none.
  • The lesson: this isn't deliberate lying — it's consistency-seeking that looks exactly like confident fabrication.
Always click the link before you trust the citation.

Key Assumptions Check

Stress-test a theory or a plan before you commit

  • State your theory plainly: then ask the model to check it against the evidence.
  • Break it into assumptions: each underlying claim is tested individually — true, false, or unproven.
  • Watch for signposts: a revenue spike tied to one-off event (like a single tournament) warns a trend may not continue.
  • Outcome: often the honest answer is a partial explanation, not a clean yes or no.

Detecting AI Content

An arms race with no fully reliable method

  • Stylometry: detectors analyze statistical patterns to guess human vs. AI — but only probabilistically.
  • False positives: non-native speakers or very formal academic writers can be misflagged as "AI-like."
  • Watermarking: new laws require labeling AI content, but watermarks are trivially removed, especially from images.
  • Geometry tells on images: AI images often break the rule that parallel lines share one vanishing point.
  • Better long-term fix: cryptographic signatures proving origin, rather than detecting AI after the fact.

Informational Heat Death

Why AI training on AI content is a slow-motion problem

  • The decay loop: models trained on AI-generated output tend to compound quality loss over generations.
  • One proposed fix: sourcing pre-AI material — old, scanned books guaranteed free of AI contamination.
  • New EU disclosure law: requires AI-generated text and images to carry some form of watermark or label.
  • Net effect: average information quality online may erode unless clean, verified sources are protected.
